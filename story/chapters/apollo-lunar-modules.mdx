There is a moment on every Apollo landing tape when the roar of the descent rocket cuts out, the lunar dust settles back to the ground like lead shot in the vacuum, and two human beings realize they are sitting on another world with no launch tower, no concrete pad, and no ground crew waiting outside. To ever see Earth again, the bottom half of their spacecraft had to perform one final, sacrificial duty: it had to stay behind and hold still in the fire.

The Apollo Lunar Module was engineered in two distinct halves: a pressurized ascent stage that housed the two astronauts, and a sturdy four-legged descent stage wrapped in amber-gold aluminized Kapton and Mylar thermal blankets. That lower stage carried the variable-thrust rocket engine, the landing radar, water and oxygen tanks, lunar science bays, and—on the final three missions—a folded electric rover tucked into a quadrant like a mechanical origami puzzle.

When Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin prepared to leave the Sea of Tranquility on July 21, 1969, there was no room for a second attempt. At the press of a button, explosive bolts fired and miniature guillotine blades sheared the thick copper umbilical cables and water lines connecting the two halves of the spacecraft in a fraction of a second.

Directly atop the descent stage's flat aluminum roof, *Eagle*'s upper cabin ignited its 3,500-pound hypergolic ascent engine. The supersonic exhaust plume slammed straight down into the lower stage's blast deflector, tearing loose shreds of gold foil and scattering them hundreds of yards across the cratered plain as the astronauts rocketed back toward orbit. Between 1969 and 1972, six of these octagonal brass-and-gold platforms were left standing across the near side of the Moon.

Welded to *Eagle*’s front landing strut, just above the ladder rung where Armstrong stepped into the dust, is a curved stainless-steel plaque bearing the signatures of the crew and the words: *“Here men from the planet Earth first set foot upon the Moon. We came in peace for all mankind.”* Around its four circular footpads, the bootprints pressed into the gray regolith in July 1969 have not blurred by a single millimeter. *Eagle* held steady in the fire so two human beings could go home—and it has kept watch over their first footsteps ever since.
