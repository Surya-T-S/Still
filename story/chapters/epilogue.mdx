From Apollo 11's *Eagle* descent stage standing in the gray stillness of Tranquility Base to Pioneer 10 coasting silently toward the red giant star Aldebaran, every retired spacecraft out there is a time capsule of human curiosity. In the airless vacuum of space, aluminum does not rust, gold foil does not tarnish, and rover tracks pressed into the dust remain crisp for millions of years.

We often call these silent craft "abandoned," as if we walked away and forgot them. Yet every single one of them was built by human hands that signed their names inside access panels, soldered their wiring under cleanroom lamps, and stayed awake through the night in Pasadena, Houston, and Maryland waiting for one last telemetry heartbeat across the dark.

They sit in patient silence across the solar system, waiting for the day our descendants walk up to their sun-bleached frames, brush the red grit off their solar wings, and read the serial numbers stamped into their metal. Until that day arrives, they are not lost—so long as someone on Earth still looks up and remembers where they are.
