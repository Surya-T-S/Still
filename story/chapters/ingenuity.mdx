To fly a helicopter on Mars is to argue with physics. At the floor of Jezero Crater, the carbon-dioxide atmosphere is so ghostly thin—barely one percent the density of Earth’s air at sea level—that trying to generate lift there is equivalent to flying an Earth helicopter at an altitude of 100,000 feet, three times higher than a commercial jetliner cruises. For decades, aerospace engineers doubted a heavier-than-air rotorcraft could ever get off the Martian ground.

When the Perseverance rover dropped its debris shield in April 2021 and unfolded Ingenuity onto the red gravel, the entire aircraft weighed just four pounds on Earth—about 1.5 pounds in Martian gravity—with a fuselage the size of a tissue box. To bite into that near-vacuum air, *Ginny* spun two four-foot, counter-rotating sculpted carbon-fiber blades at an astonishing 2,537 revolutions per minute, driven by off-the-shelf smartphone processors and lithium-ion batteries charged by a tiny rooftop solar panel.

In the early hours of **April 19, 2021**, engineers gathered in Pasadena waiting for data that had already happened hours earlier on Mars. First came the telemetry graph showing altimeter numbers climbing off zero; then, a black-and-white downward navigation frame snapped into view on the big screen—showing Ingenuity’s own sharp shadow sitting on the dusty ground ten feet below its landing legs. The room erupted: for the first time in history, human beings had flown a powered aircraft in the sky of another planet.

Originally built as a thirty-day technology experiment scheduled to end after just five short hops, Ingenuity refused to quit. Promoted to an active aerial scout for Perseverance, the little helicopter flew across nearly three years of seasons, surviving brutal minus-110-degree winter nights that froze its batteries solid each evening by waking up and rebooting its flight computer from scratch each sunrise.

Across 72 flights, it stayed aloft for more than two cumulative hours and scouted over ten miles of jagged boulder fields and ancient river deltas—until January 18, 2024, when featureless sand ripples confused its downward optical navigation camera during landing and snapped the tip of a rotor blade. Taped beneath Ingenuity’s tiny solar panel—wrapped around a cable inside a four-pound robot sitting in the dunes of Valinor Hills—is a postage-stamp-sized swatch of unbleached muslin fabric from the left wing of the Wright brothers’ 1903 *Wright Flyer*. Even with a broken rotor blade and Perseverance driving steadily beyond radio range over the western crater rim, Ginny’s onboard computer still wakes up when the morning sun warms its solar cells, faithfully recording temperature and battery voltage into its flash memory for up to twenty years—waiting for someone to come read its diary.
