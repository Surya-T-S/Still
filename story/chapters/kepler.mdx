For thousands of years, human beings stared up at the night sky and wondered a single, haunting question: are the stars lonely campfires in an empty wilderness, or are they surrounded by worlds like our own? Before the Kepler Space Telescope launched in March 2009, astronomers had found a few hundred gas giants, yet nobody knew with statistical certainty whether small, rocky, Earth-sized planets were commonplace or a cosmic fluke.

To find out, Kepler did something no ground telescope blinded by Earth's shimmering atmosphere could ever do: it slipped into a quiet heliocentric orbit trailing behind Earth and stared without blinking at a single patch of sky in the constellations Cygnus and Lyra for four straight years. Inside its barrel sat a curved 95-megapixel array of 42 charge-coupled sensors monitoring the brightness of more than 150,000 stars simultaneously every thirty minutes.

The telescope was looking for shadows almost too small to believe. Whenever an orbiting planet crossed directly in front of its host star—a transit—the star's light dimmed by less than one part in ten thousand, comparable to watching a single flea crawl across a car headlight from miles away.

Even when two of Kepler’s four mechanical reaction wheels failed in 2013—threatening to end the mission—engineers invented a breathtaking rescue called *K2*, balancing the telescope against the gentle physical pressure of sunlight photons striking its roof like a sailboat keeled against the wind. Across nine and a half years and more than half a million observed stars, Kepler discovered over 2,600 confirmed exoplanets: worlds of lava, worlds orbiting twin suns, and rocky worlds circling inside the temperate habitable zones of their stars.

In October 2018, ninety-four million miles behind Earth, Kepler’s hydrazine fuel tank finally ran dry. By a quiet coincidence of history, NASA transmitted the final “goodnight” command to shut down Kepler’s radio transmitter on November 15, 2018—the 388th anniversary of the death of seventeenth-century astronomer Johannes Kepler. Before its eyes closed in its eternal orbit around the Sun, this solitary brass-and-graphite barrel changed how every human being looks up at the dark: we now know that there are more planets than stars in the Milky Way.
