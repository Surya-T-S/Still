For thousands of years, human beings stared up at the night sky and wondered a single, haunting question: are the stars lonely campfires in an empty wilderness, or are they surrounded by worlds like our own? Before the Kepler Space Telescope launched in March 2009, astronomers had found a few hundred gas giants, yet nobody knew with statistical certainty whether small, rocky, Earth-sized planets were commonplace or a cosmic fluke.

For decades, senior review boards had rejected the mission proposal as physically impossible: to spot an Earth-sized planet crossing in front of a distant star required measuring brightness changes of less than one part in ten thousand—comparable to standing miles away from a skyscraper and detecting a single person lowering a window blind by half an inch. Undeterred, principal investigator William Borucki spent years in laboratories proving that silicon charge-coupled detectors could hold that steady in the cold of space.

Slipping into a quiet heliocentric orbit trailing behind Earth, Kepler stared without blinking at a single patch of sky in the constellations Cygnus and Lyra for four straight years. Inside its barrel sat a curved 95-megapixel array of 42 sensors monitoring the brightness of more than 150,000 stars simultaneously every thirty minutes, waiting for the tiny, rhythmic blink of an alien world passing across its sun.

Even when two of Kepler’s four mechanical reaction wheels seized up in 2013—threatening to end the mission—engineers invented a breathtaking rescue called *K2*, balancing the telescope against the gentle physical pressure of sunlight photons striking its angled roof like a sailboat keeled against the wind. Across nine and a half years and more than half a million observed stars, Kepler discovered over 2,600 confirmed exoplanets: worlds of molten lava, worlds with two suns in their sky like Tatooine, and rocky worlds circling inside the temperate habitable zones where liquid water can pool.

In October 2018, ninety-four million miles behind Earth, Kepler’s hydrazine fuel tank finally sputtered dry. By a quiet coincidence of history, NASA transmitted the final “goodnight” command to shut down Kepler’s radio transmitter on **November 15, 2018**—the 388th anniversary of the death of seventeenth-century astronomer Johannes Kepler. Before its eyes closed in its eternal orbit around the Sun, this solitary brass-and-graphite barrel changed how every human being looks up at the dark: we now know that there are more planets than stars in the Milky Way.
