Imagine walking across an uncharted mountain range while carrying your own atmosphere on your back, knowing that every step burns precious minutes of cooling water and oxygen—and that if you twist an ankle a mile from shelter, no rescue helicopter can ever reach you. Before the arrival of the Lunar Roving Vehicle on Apollo 15, astronauts were restricted to exploring on foot within a strict walking radius of their landers, exhausted by the strange, loping rhythm of one-sixth gravity.

To break that tether, engineers at Boeing and General Motors designed a car that could be folded like a suitcase into a triangular bay only five feet wide. When the astronauts pulled two nylon lanyard ribbons on the lunar surface, torsion bars and springs unfolded the ten-foot aluminum chassis onto the dust in less than twenty minutes. Because rubber tires would have frozen brittle or burst in the vacuum, each wheel was woven from zinc-coated piano wire with titanium chevrons riveted to the tread.

Each 460-pound rover used four quarter-horsepower electric hub motors powered by two 36-volt non-rechargeable silver-zinc batteries. Because stiff pressurized gloves made turning a steering wheel impossible, the crew drove with a single T-shaped aircraft joystick—pushing forward to accelerate, pulling back to brake, and tilting left or right to steer all four wheels through powdery craters.

Over Apollo 15, 16, and 17, the three buggies covered more than 56 cumulative miles of lunar terrain, climbing steep mountain flanks above Hadley Rille and hauling hundreds of pounds of ancient volcanic rock. When each mission ended, the astronauts drove their rover roughly 300 feet east of the lander, engaged the parking brake, and aligned the umbrella-shaped high-gain antenna straight toward Earth so Mission Control could watch them leave.

On December 14, 1972, Apollo 17 commander Gene Cernan—the last human to walk on the Moon—drove LRV-3 out to its final parking spot in the Taurus-Littrow valley, brushed the grey dust off its fenders one last time, and placed a small red-on-silver plaque between the two lawn-chair seats. Hours later, controlled remotely by a single engineer in Houston with a 1.3-second delay, the rover’s television camera tilted upward to watch Cernan’s spacecraft rocket into the black sky. The camera panned down to the empty valley floor, and then went dark—leaving two parallel tire tracks stretching across the Moon with no one left to follow them.
