There is a bold line in aerospace engineering between an orbiter and a lander. An orbiter is a delicate chandelier of fragile solar paddles, exposed star trackers, and rigid antennas built to float forever in weightlessness; it has no shock absorbers, no landing legs, and no crushable footpads. The Near Earth Asteroid Rendezvous—renamed NEAR Shoemaker in honor of planetary geologist Eugene Shoemaker—was strictly an orbiter.

Even reaching asteroid 433 Eros nearly cost the mission everything. In December 1998, an aborted main-engine burn tumbled the spacecraft into an emergency spin, bleeding away precious pounds of fuel and delaying arrival by more than a year. Yet navigators salvaged the trajectory, and on Valentine's Day 2000, NEAR Shoemaker slipped into orbit around Eros—a tumbling, twenty-one-mile-long potato-shaped boulder of ancient silicate rock—mapping 160,000 photographs of its craters and ridges from altitudes of 20 to 200 miles.

By February 2001, its fuel tank was nearly empty and its orbital mission was complete. Rather than simply switching the spacecraft off in orbit, mission director Robert Farquhar and engineers at the Johns Hopkins Applied Physics Laboratory proposed a daring finale: what if they tried to bring a legless orbiter all the way down to the surface using a series of carefully timed braking burns? Even if the spacecraft snapped a solar wing on contact, its camera would capture the highest-resolution images of an asteroid ever seen on the way down.

On **February 12, 2001**, across nearly two hundred million miles of space, five small thruster burns slowed NEAR Shoemaker’s descent against Eros’s vanishingly weak gravity to a gentle walk of 1.5 meters per second—less than four miles per hour. As the spacecraft dropped through the final five hundred feet, its camera snapped sixty-nine increasingly sharp photographs—the last frame cut off mid-transmission at an altitude of just 390 feet, showing pebbles the size of fists resting in an ancient regolith pond.

Inside the control room in Laurel, Maryland, engineers expected the radio carrier wave to vanish the instant metal struck rock. Instead, at 3:01 p.m. Eastern time, the tips of two solar panels and the bottom rim of the spacecraft brushed into the dusty floor of a saddle-shaped depression, came to a stop without bouncing, and kept right on transmitting a faint, steady carrier tone.

Astonishingly, the orbiter survived touchdown completely intact. Reprogramming its gamma-ray spectrometer to use the asteroid dirt directly beneath its belly as a target, NASA gathered sixteen more days of elemental surface readings before the deep cold of an asteroid winter finally closed the link. Leaning quietly on two tips of its fixed solar panels in a gravity two thousand times weaker than Earth’s, the little orbiter still rides 433 Eros as it tumbles end over end around the Sun.
