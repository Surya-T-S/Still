There is a bold line in aerospace engineering between an orbiter and a lander. An orbiter is a delicate chandelier of fragile solar paddles, exposed star trackers, and rigid antennas built to float forever in weightlessness; it has no shock absorbers, no landing legs, and no crushable footpads. The Near Earth Asteroid Rendezvous—renamed NEAR Shoemaker in honor of planetary geologist Eugene Shoemaker—was strictly an orbiter.

For an entire year starting on Valentine's Day 2000, NEAR Shoemaker circled asteroid 433 Eros—a tumbling, twenty-one-mile-long potato-shaped boulder of ancient silicate rock—from altitudes of 20 to 200 miles. It mapped every crater, ridge, and regolith boulder on Eros, transmitting 160,000 photographs and proving that the asteroid was a solid, fractured remnant of the early solar system. By February 2001, its fuel tank was nearly empty and its orbital mission was complete.

Rather than simply switching the spacecraft off in orbit, mission director Robert Farquhar and engineers at the Johns Hopkins Applied Physics Laboratory proposed a daring finale: what if they tried to bring a legless orbiter all the way down to the surface using a series of carefully timed braking burns? Even if the spacecraft snapped a solar wing on contact, its camera would capture the highest-resolution images of an asteroid ever seen on the way down.

On February 12, 2001, across nearly two hundred million miles of space, five small thruster burns slowed NEAR Shoemaker’s descent against Eros’s vanishingly weak gravity to a gentle walk of 1.5 meters per second—less than four miles per hour. At 3:01 p.m. Eastern time, the tips of two solar panels and the bottom rim of the spacecraft brushed into the dusty floor of a saddle-shaped depression and came to a stop without even bouncing.

As NEAR Shoemaker dropped through the final five hundred feet, its camera snapped sixty-nine increasingly sharp photographs—the last frame cut off mid-transmission at an altitude of just 390 feet, showing pebbles the size of fists resting in an ancient regolith pond. Astonishingly, the spacecraft survived completely intact, transmitting gamma-ray soil readings from the ground for sixteen more days. Leaning quietly on two tips of its fixed solar panels in a gravity two thousand times weaker than Earth’s, the little orbiter still rides 433 Eros as it tumbles end over end around the Sun.
