Rovers get the glory of roving across horizons, but some secrets can only be uncovered by standing utterly still. To find out whether water ice still hides beneath the Martian dirt—and whether the planet's deep interior is dead stone or a living, molten engine—NASA sent two stationary sentinels a decade apart: Phoenix to the freezing northern arctic in 2008, and InSight to the quiet equatorial plains in 2018.

Touchdown for Phoenix in the far northern Vastitas Borealis was a race against the calendar. When its robotic backhoe scraped away just two inches of reddish topsoil in a trench dubbed *“Dodo-Goldilocks,”* its camera revealed bright white chunks at the bottom of the groove. Skeptics initially wondered if the white material was merely salt—until four days later, when a follow-up photograph showed that several of the dice-sized crumbs had vanished completely into thin air, sublimating into water vapor in the sunlight and proving that pure water ice lies just inches beneath the northern soil.

Phoenix worked frantically for five months until the encroaching polar winter plunged the region into continuous darkness and entombed the lander in a heavy seasonal shell of solid carbon-dioxide dry ice. Ten years later, using the exact same spacecraft hull architecture, InSight touched down on the flat lava plains of Elysium Planitia for an entirely different kind of listening.

With a delicate robotic grapple, InSight lifted a copper-colored dome—the SEIS seismometer—off its deck and placed it directly onto the ground, pressing an ear against the chest of the planet. Shielded from the wind beneath a chainmail-and-foil dome, that instrument measured ground vibrations smaller than the width of a single hydrogen atom. Over four Earth years, InSight felt 1,319 marsquakes and the shockwaves of distant meteoroids slamming into the crust—timing the seismic echoes to map the thickness of the Martian mantle and prove that Mars still holds a vast, liquid iron-alloy core roughly half the radius of the planet itself.

Yet Elysium Planitia offered none of the lucky dust devils that had saved Spirit and Opportunity. Month after month, a velvet blanket of red dust thickened over InSight’s two circular solar fans until daily electricity fell by more than ninety percent. In its final weeks in **December 2022**, when InSight had barely enough current left to keep a single system alive, engineers chose *not* to run the survival heaters that would have kept the lander warm. Instead, they poured every remaining drop of solar energy into the seismometer on the ground beside it—listening to the faint, lonely tremors of the planet’s heart until the very last volt was gone.
