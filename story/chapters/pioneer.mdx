Before 1972, no human-made object had ever ventured beyond the orbit of Mars. Between the inner rocky planets and the giant gas worlds lay the main asteroid belt—a vast ring of tumbling rock and dust that some scientists feared might shred any spacecraft attempting to cross it—and beyond that waited Jupiter’s ferocious magnetic radiation belts, strong enough to fry unshielded electronics in minutes.

Pioneer 10 and its twin, Pioneer 11, were built to run that gauntlet first. Shaped like nine-foot aluminum satellite dishes with long magnetometer booms and nuclear SNAP-19 plutonium generators extended on struts, they spun like gyroscopes at five revolutions per minute to keep their antennas locked on Earth. Launched atop Atlas-Centaur rockets in 1972 and 1973, they emerged unscathed from the asteroid belt, swept past Jupiter’s swirling Great Red Spot, and—in Pioneer 11’s case—threaded beneath Saturn’s icy rings, blazing the trail for Voyager 1 and 2.

The gravitational slingshot that hurled Pioneer 10 past Jupiter in December 1973 accelerated the 570-pound probe beyond solar escape velocity—meaning it would never return to the Sun, becoming the first artifact of human civilization to be cast out into the Milky Way galaxy. Realizing what that meant, astronomer Carl Sagan, Frank Drake, and artist Linda Salzman Sagan had three weeks before launch to design a six-by-nine-inch gold-anodized aluminum plaque bolted to each spacecraft's antenna struts.

Engraved into that golden plate is a cosmic bottle cast into the interstellar ocean: a diagram of the spin-flip transition of neutral hydrogen, the fourteen pulsing neutron stars that triangulate the location and epoch of our Sun, a schematic of our solar system showing the probe's path from the third planet, and the unadorned figures of a man and a woman standing in front of the spacecraft's silhouette.

For more than thirty years, as Pioneer 10 coasted past the orbits of Uranus, Neptune, and Pluto, its decaying plutonium thermocouples grew colder and its radio voice fainter—until January 23, 2003, when a 70-meter dish in Madrid caught one final, barely audible sigh from 7.6 billion miles away. Long after our oceans boil away under an aging Sun five billion years from now, Pioneer 10 and 11 will still be gliding through the quiet between the stars, their gold plaques unmarred by time, carrying an open hand raised in greeting from a small blue world third from the Sun.
