Look up at the night sky on a clear evening, and everything appears ancient, untamed, and untouched by human hands. Yet scattered across the gray basalt plains of the Moon, buried beneath the rust-colored dunes of Mars, and coasting through the black emptiness between the planets sits a quiet, solitary fleet of machines we built and never brought home.

For over six decades, robotic probes and crewed landers have departed Earth on one-way journeys. They were never designed to return. Every gram of return fuel would have meant one less camera, one less seismometer, or one less human heartbeat making it safely back to Earth. Once their silver-zinc batteries depleted, their solar panels disappeared beneath fine red dust, or their hydrazine tanks ran dry, they remained in the exact posture of their final working second.

Yet if you could stand beside any one of them right now, you would not feel like you were looking at scrap metal. You would see switches still flipped to the positions where gloved fingers left them in **December 1972**, rover tracks curving around boulders as if their drivers stepped away five minutes ago, and golden dishes still craned toward a pale blue star in their sky—waiting for a command that stopped coming.

As you move outward through STILL, the radio signal delay from Earth stretches in real time—from a second and a half at the Moon, to twenty minutes across the Martian desert, to hours in the deep cold beyond the outer planets. None of these machines failed us. Every one of them kept transmitting until the physics of darkness, dust, or distance finally closed its eyes. Step closer, and listen to what they saw before their signals faded.
