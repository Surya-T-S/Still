On July 4, 1997, a twenty-three-pound aluminum box no larger than a kitchen microwave sat tucked inside a cluster of deflated airbags on a rocky flood plain called Ares Vallis. For twenty years since the Viking landings, every attempt to reach the Martian surface had ended in silence or failure. Nobody knew if a six-wheeled robot could actually roll off a lander and survive the jagged volcanic boulders and minus-100-degree nights of Mars.

Even on the first day after landing, suspense gripped the control room in Pasadena: one of Pathfinder’s deflated airbags had bunched up right at the foot of the rover’s deployment ramp, threatening to snag Sojourner’s wheels before she could even touch the dirt. Engineers held their breath as the lander raised its petal, ironed the fabric flat, and watched the little rover roll down the steel tracks—inch by cautious inch—until all six wheels rested in the red dust of another planet.

Built as a high-risk, low-cost engineering experiment with an initial warranty of just seven Martian days, Sojourner was a triumph of miniature ingenuity. Its flat back was tiled with gallium-arsenide solar cells, its belly insulated with ultra-light silica aerogel—"frozen smoke" weighing scarcely more than air—and its six cleaved aluminum wheels mounted on a rocker-bogie suspension that could climb right over rocks twice their own diameter without tipping the chassis.

Operating with an 8-bit Intel 80C85 processor running at a humble 2 MHz—less computing power than a pocket calculator—Sojourner moved at a deliberate one centimeter per second. Because radio signals took more than ten minutes to cross the gulf between Earth and Mars, human drivers in Pasadena could never steer her in real time; instead, they wore red-and-blue 3D glasses to study stereo photographs each night and uploaded a careful sequence of turns, waiting until the next morning to see if she had cleared the boulders.

Over 83 sols—nearly twelve times her planned lifetime—Sojourner traversed 330 feet around the landing site, pressing her Alpha Proton X-ray Spectrometer nose-first against rocks nicknamed *“Barnacle Bill,”* *“Yogi,”* and *“Shark.”* Then, on **September 27, 1997**, the stationary Pathfinder base lander suffered a fatal battery failure in the extreme cold, cutting off the radio relay that connected Sojourner to Earth.

What makes Sojourner’s ending so deeply poignant is her final contingency programming: the little rover was instructed that if she ever went several days without hearing from Earth through the Pathfinder base station, she should drive back toward the lander and circle slowly around its perimeter—calling out on her short-range UHF radio, waiting for a reply from a home station that had already frozen solid in the Martian night.
