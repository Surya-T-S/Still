When Spirit and Opportunity bounced down onto opposite sides of Mars in January 2004, mission planners at the Jet Propulsion Laboratory gave them a strict life expectancy: ninety Martian days. Within three months, engineers calculated, fine iron-oxide dust settling out of the salmon-colored sky would coat their flat solar wings like rust-colored frost, starving their batteries until they froze to death in the dark.

Neither the engineers nor the rovers anticipated that Mars itself would lend a hand. Again and passing again, whirlwind dust devils—spinning columns of thin Martian air—swept across the plains at just the right moment, scrubbing the glass solar arrays clean and surging power back into their batteries in miraculous "cleaning events" that turned a three-month sprint into a fourteen-year odyssey.

Equipped with panoramic mast cameras, diamond-tipped rock abrasion grinders, microscopic imagers, and Mössbauer spectrometers, the twin golf-cart-sized geologists rewrote the history of the Red Planet. At Gusev Crater, when Spirit’s right-front wheel seized up in 2006, drivers turned the rover around and drove her backward for years; that dragging wheel accidentally gouged a bright white trench in the soil, exposing 90% pure opaline silica formed by ancient volcanic hot springs. Half a world away on Meridiani Planum, Opportunity discovered millions of tiny gray hematite spheres—nicknamed *“blueberries”*—that could only have precipitated inside ancient, mineral-rich liquid water.

Spirit finally became trapped in soft hidden sulfate sand at a spot called *Troy* in 2009, tilting away from the winter sun until her heaters fell silent in 2010. Opportunity kept rolling alone across forty-five kilometers of desert—an off-world marathon record—until June 2018, when a monster, planet-encircling dust storm swallowed the entire globe, blotting out 99% of sunlight over Perseverance Valley.

For eight months after Opportunity’s final packet—which reported an atmospheric opacity so thick that midday looked like midnight and solar array energy had fallen to just 22 watt-hours—engineers at JPL beamed more than a thousand recovery commands and wakeup songs across the dark. On the night of February 12, 2019, inside the hushed Space Flight Operations Facility, flight controllers transmitted Billie Holiday’s *“I’ll Be Seeing You”* across 140 million miles of vacuum, waited through the twenty-minute silence for an answer that never came, and finally let the fourteen-year marathon runner rest.
