In the early 1960s, a terrifying hypothesis haunted the hallways of NASA: what if four billion years of micrometeoroid impacts had ground the lunar surface into an electrostatic quicksand hundreds of feet deep? If the theory was right, the moment an Apollo lander touched down, it would sink out of sight into a bottomless sea of powder, entombed before the crew could even unbuckle their harnesses.

Someone had to touch the ground first. Between 1966 and 1968, NASA dispatched the Surveyor series—spidery, three-legged aluminum tripods engineered to perform the first true soft landings on the Moon. On April 20, 1967, Surveyor 3 dropped toward the Ocean of Storms, its radar bouncing confused signals off the bright rocks of a crater rim. Instead of cutting its vernier thrusters at touchdown, the lander bounced twice across the inner slope of the crater before coming to rest at a tilt.

Equipped with an accordion-hinged pantograph arm, a miniature steel scoop, and a rotating mirror television camera, Surveyor 3 dug trenches up to seven inches deep, pinched clods of lunar soil until they crumbled, and transmitted more than 6,300 photographs before the first two-week lunar night froze its battery solid. It proved beyond doubt that the Moon resembled damp beach sand—firm enough to hold the weight of astronauts.

Then, thirty-one months after Surveyor 3 went cold, the sky above its quiet crater lit up with rocket fire. On November 19, 1969, Apollo 12 astronauts Pete Conrad and Alan Bean executed a pinpoint landing just 535 feet away on the crater's rim—close enough that lunar dust kicked up by their descent engine sandblasted Surveyor's flank.

To this day, Surveyor 3 remains the only spacecraft launched from Earth to ever be visited by human beings on another celestial body. Its white paint had been tanned a pale golden-brown by two and a half years of unfiltered solar radiation, waiting quietly on the slope of a shallow crater until Conrad and Bean hopped down the rim, patted its aluminum frame like an old friend, and unbolted its 22-pound television camera with handheld shears to bring back to Houston—proving that the machines we send ahead of us are never truly unreachable.
